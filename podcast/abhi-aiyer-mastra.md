# What Abhi Aiyer taught me about making moves in AI tooling

*Dev Propulsion Labs · December 2025*

I'm Victoria Melnikova, and I sat down with Abhi Aiyer on his birthday for Dev Propulsion Labs to talk about how Mastra bet everything on TypeScript for AI agents, turned a free book into a viral growth engine, and raised their seed round exclusively from builders. He's blunt and funny, and he has zero patience for "that's cool" feedback.

- YouTube: https://youtu.be/ORARUuQ-I9I
- Show notes: https://evilmartians.com/events/abhi-aiyer-co-founder-of-mastra-typescript-for-ai-agents-and-making-moves

## When "that's cool" means go away

Before Mastra became Mastra, it was an N8N-style visual agent builder. Abhi and his co-founders showed it to friends and enemies. (He insists you have to ask your enemies too.) Everyone said: "Oh, that's interesting. That's cool."

Abhi was distraught, because "that's cool" is the polite version of "go away." It's the thumbs-up reaction on your message from someone who couldn't be bothered to type three letters.

> "If they just say 'that's cool,' that really just means go away. You want actual feedback. Like they hate it, or look where you're going."

Nobody gave real feedback, so Abhi had to work out for himself why they hated it. Every developer they'd talked to wanted full control of the code: TypeScript instead of drag-and-drop. Mastra pivoted, and it worked. I see the same thing with the startups I work with. A lukewarm response is the most dangerous kind, because it feels like a signal and tells you nothing.

## Python was just making HTTP calls the whole time

I asked Abhi why TypeScript for AI, and his answer made me laugh. He looked at what Python developers were actually doing with AI and found no special ML libraries doing the heavy lifting, only HTTP calls to OpenAI.

> "I look into all this stuff and it's like, you're making an HTTP call to OpenAI. This doesn't need to be anything special. JavaScript developers already do HTTP calls. And we have better packages for a lot of stuff."

The Mastra team had been TypeScript builders their entire careers, and the week before our conversation, TypeScript became the most popular language on GitHub. Abhi thinks every web developer using React will soon be building agents without even thinking about it. Python's dominance in AI is a historical artifact from the data science era, and when the underlying technology is API calls, the framework that fits your existing workflow wins.

## The book that did what marketing couldn't

Mastra's co-founder Sam wrote a free book on the principles of AI engineering, and it went viral. People who don't even use JavaScript like it. Enterprise leads pick it up at conferences through guerrilla marketing and enter the funnel from there, while developers find the framework first and read the book as supplementary education.

So the book brings in enterprise leads from the top, and the framework pulls in developers from the bottom. The second book, Patterns, covers recipes for assembling the building blocks: once you have all the Lego pieces, you need to figure out how to put them together.

I saw stacks of Mastra books myself at TED AI in San Francisco, which isn't a typical developer conference. I've been watching education-first marketing closely across devtools, and Mastra is one of the best examples I've seen. They also run weekly livestreams on Mondays and workshops on Thursdays.

## 120 builders on the cap table, zero accidents

When the Mastra co-founders sat down to write why they were starting a company, the answers were hilariously honest. Abhi: work with friends and make moves. One co-founder: become the richest guy in South Dakota. (He's not there yet.)

They decided every investor should be a builder, and anyone who'd never written a line of code shouldn't bother talking to them. The result was a coalition round with 120+ builder-investors, including Paul Graham.

> "We thought we should raise all of our money from builders. If you didn't write a lick of code in your life, you shouldn't talk to us or give us money or anything."

Most startups take whatever money they can get, so I loved how aggressively Mastra filtered for cultural fit on the investor side. Abhi hires the same way, by building friendships over time instead of pitching jobs. His team of 18 includes people he's known for years across multiple companies, and he's even hired their brothers. The whole culture runs on trust networks that existed before the company did, with Abhi as the connector.

## Write the docs so you never have to talk about it again

Abhi says plainly that he hates writing documentation. But he reframed it in a way that stuck with me: every good doc page means one less person who has to talk to you, one less GitHub issue, and one less management cycle of being mad about the same thing.

> "Just fix the docs so they never have to talk to you about it. Optimize for not talking to people. That's what I do."

The Mastra community roasts their docs constantly on Discord, and Abhi treats that as actionable work instead of getting defensive. Docs and guides get the same priority as bug fixes. When several people complain about the same thing, that's the signal to fix it for good. It's the most pragmatic approach to documentation I've heard from a founder: he writes docs because he'd love to stop having the same conversation over and over.

## YC's last slide made him laugh out loud

Abhi went into YC as a skeptic from LA who didn't think San Francisco mattered. His dev tools cohort was all "old people": 30+, with kids. They put all the adults together, and that turned out to be a strength: more life experience, less ego, and deep friendships that outlasted the program.

On the last day of YC, they showed a slide: 99.99% of successful companies are in the Bay Area. It confirmed exactly what Abhi had come to believe during the batch. (They brainwashed him, he says. But not really.)

> "I started laughing. I laughed out loud and the people around me looked. I was like, I'm sorry. I just laughed because I was right. They didn't tell us specifically. But that is exactly what you see."

Everything at Mastra comes back to something Abhi learned from Brad Flora at YC: generosity breeds luck. Ask anyone from Mastra for help and they'll help, no strings attached. Co-founder Shane personally emails users to check in. When I told Abhi that Shane had reached out to us at Evil Martians because we were using Mastra, he just nodded. The sarcasm is real, and so is the generosity. I talk to dozens of devtools founders every year, and in an ecosystem that can feel transactional, that generosity stands out.
