# What David Gomes taught me about why half of developers still aren't using AI

*Dev Propulsion Labs · February 2026*

I'm Victoria Melnikova, and this is my conversation with David Gomes, a product engineer who's worked inside SingleStore, Neon, and now Cursor. We talked about how unevenly AI coding has spread, building an engineering team in Portugal, and why he went back to being an IC.

## The future is here, just wildly uneven

When I moved to SF in March 2025, there seemed to be new terms and new models every single day. But many developers outside this bubble (and I talk to a lot of them through Dev Propulsion Labs) haven't even tried agentic coding. David confirmed what I'd been sensing: around 50% of the world's software engineers still aren't using agentic coding tools in any meaningful way.

Meanwhile, some of the best engineers around, like Jose Valim and Jared Sumner, are producing most of their code through agents. The tools aren't magic yet: they take practice, prompting skill, and an understanding of rules, skills, and MCPs. David sees the other half as a massive market still to capture, which is exactly how someone at Cursor should think about it.

> "It's basically like a new thing that you have to learn as a software engineer. It takes practice. It takes some amount of skill."

I've seen this firsthand with the engineers I work with at Evil Martians. The senior developers who pushed back hardest at first are getting the best results now that they've put in the reps.

## Even inside Cursor, nobody codes the same way

This surprised me. You'd think the people building the leading AI code editor would converge on one workflow. Nope. Some engineers at Cursor live in the CLI, and others run cloud agents from the web UI or their phone. David uses Cursor's Best-of-N feature, launching three Codex and three Opus models on the same task and picking the best output.

He doesn't expect that to change anytime soon, and I think he's right. Software engineers have always customized their environments obsessively, and AI coding is no different. For devtools companies, that means building for the many ways people actually work instead of forcing one workflow.

## The competitive programming kid who built a 50-person team back home

David grew up doing competitive programming in Portugal, dreamed of moving to SF since high school, and got there right after university. At SingleStore, he convinced the company to open an engineering office in Portugal and grew it from zero to 50 people, which I really admire.

His case for Portugal as a first European office is practical: the right time zone (eight hours from SF), exceptional English skills (because the Portuguese don't dub TV, seriously, that's the reason), and a lower cost of living than Ireland or the UK. He believes his team shifted the salary market in Lisbon for top-tier engineers. Before SingleStore, not many US companies were hiring locally. Now CloudFlare, Datadog, PagerDuty, and Docker all have offices there.

I lived in Lisbon for a few years, so I loved hearing this. Portugal has incredible talent, and it needed someone to open the door.

## Inside Neon's billion-dollar exit to Databricks

David worked at Neon under Nikita Shamgunov, his second time working for Nikita after SingleStore. He describes Neon's product as "Postgres, but easy and cheap." Revenue was growing like a rocket, and the $1B acquisition by Databricks was, in his words, "one for the books."

> "The value that Neon was acquired for is just insane. $1 billion for a company that had been created just a few years before the exit."

He credits Nikita with negotiating to keep Neon as its own brand and product after the acquisition, which isn't common. I talked to Stas Kelvich from Neon on a later episode of Dev Propulsion Labs, and it really was an unusual deal structure. Databricks wanting to keep Neon intact says a lot about how strong its product-led growth was.

## Not everyone needs to care about the business, and that's fine

I asked David whether engineers should understand business goals, and his answer wasn't the one I expected. He used to get frustrated when engineers didn't care about competitors or revenue, but he's since learned that teams work best when they're diverse in orientation.

Some people are obsessed with the technology, and others focus on the product and market. His approach is to point each person's energy at work that matches their strengths. More engineering leaders need to hear this. I've seen too many startups try to turn every engineer into a mini product manager, and it doesn't work.

## The engineer-manager pendulum, and why his next company will be his own

David pointed to Charity Majors' blog post about alternating between IC and management roles over a career, and he's done exactly that: IC to manager at SingleStore, IC to manager at Neon, and now IC at Cursor. He chose to be an IC right now because he wants to put in reps on AI coding before managing again.

When I asked about starting his own company, he said something that made me laugh: "My next company is gonna be my own, but I just keep getting offers I can't refuse." Cursor was one of those offers. He was already using the product all day long and had always wanted to work on IDEs.

My takeaway: half the world's engineers haven't adopted AI coding, and the ones who have keep pulling further ahead. If you're still on the fence, put in the reps, because this is a skill you can learn.
