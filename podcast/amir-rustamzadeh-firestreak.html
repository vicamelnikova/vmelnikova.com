What Amir Rustamzadeh taught me about what VCs really look for in devtools founders

Amir Rustamzadeh is a partner at Firestreak Ventures. He got there through aerospace at JPL and SpaceX, then startups, and he helped shape developer experience as a category during his time at Cypress. So when he talks about what makes a developer tool fundable, he's speaking as someone who built one before he started writing checks.

From JPL and SpaceX to venture capital

I'm Victoria Melnikova, and I don't get to talk to many investors who started their careers building spacecraft. Amir worked in aerospace, including JPL and SpaceX, before moving into startups and then venture capital at Firestreak. You can hear the engineer in how he evaluates founders: he looks for the load-bearing parts and the failure modes rather than the marketing.

Developer experience as a category he helped build

Before investing, Amir was at Cypress while developer experience was turning into a real category. He's been through the same motion his founders are attempting: earning developer trust, turning it into adoption, and then figuring out whether that adoption can become a durable business. When he hears a devtools pitch, he's comparing it to work he's done himself.

Founders before products

The thing I most wanted his read on, and the thread through our whole conversation, is that he backs founders before products. Early-stage products change constantly, so betting on today's version is a mistake. He's underwriting the founder's ability to keep learning and making good calls as the market moves. Most serious early-stage investors say some version of this, but Amir was unusually concrete about why the founder is the real asset.

Why most developer tools never reach venture scale

Most developer tools struggle to reach venture scale, and Amir and I didn't pretend otherwise. Plenty of them are good businesses, but a good business and a venture-scale outcome are different things, and mixing them up burns founders and investors alike. We went through why: crowded categories, buyers who are hard to charge, and the gap between developer love and enterprise revenue. If you're raising, be clear with yourself about which of the two you're building.

Breaking through a crowded market

We also talked about Lovable and Daytona, two teams that broke through crowded markets. My takeaway was that distribution and timing often matter as much as the product, and the founders who win treat go-to-market as a first-class problem from day one.
