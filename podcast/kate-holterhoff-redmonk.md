# What Kate Holterhoff taught me about AI slop, agent experience, and the open source crisis

*Dev Propulsion Labs · July 2026 · Guest: Kate Holterhoff, Senior Analyst at RedMonk · Host: Victoria Melnikova*

Kate Holterhoff is a senior analyst at RedMonk, and her path to developer infrastructure research is one of the most unusual I've come across: mural painting, a literature PhD, a Georgia Tech postdoc, and frontend engineering, all before she started analyzing the tools we build on. Because of that range, our conversation went places most industry talk doesn't.

## An analyst who came up through paint, literature, and frontend code

I'm Victoria Melnikova, and I spend most of my time with founders, so sitting down with an analyst was a deliberate change of angle. Kate painted murals in Cincinnati, did her literature PhD at Carnegie Mellon and a postdoc at Georgia Tech, and worked as a frontend engineer before spending the last four years covering developer infrastructure. I came away thinking the best analysis of our industry comes from people who've lived on both sides of it: she can read the code, and she can read the culture around it.

We talked about what analyst work at RedMonk actually involves, and how AI is pushing every research and consulting business to rethink what it sells. If anyone can get a summary of a technology from a prompt, what's left to sell is judgment and context. I see the same shift hitting devtools companies, not only the analysts who cover them.

## The open source crisis: AI slop and the review bottleneck

What stayed with me most was Kate's read on open source. Maintainers are drowning in low-quality, AI-generated pull requests, so reviewing code, rather than writing it, has become the bottleneck. Anyone can generate a plausible-looking PR in seconds, and a human still has to spend real attention evaluating it.

Some projects are responding by closing the door. tldraw and Ladybird have limited external contributions, which open source maintainers would once have found almost unthinkable. It makes sense as a defense, but it also changes what "open" means in practice.

## Supply chain threats and the cost of wider access

We also got into supply chain attacks like Mini Shai-Hulud and how they've reached projects such as TanStack. Kate kept coming back to one idea: every time you open a tool up to more people, whether less technical users or automated agents, you take on new security tradeoffs. The teams that treat security as part of their developer experience are the ones people still trust when something goes wrong.

## Agent experience as a new discovery channel

We finished on agent experience as a new way developer tools get discovered. If agents are the ones reading your docs, calling your APIs, and choosing between competing products, then whether an agent can make sense of your product becomes a distribution question.

Underneath all of it was RedMonk's community-first approach, the one behind Monktoberfest and Monki Gras. That felt right for this show, since so much of what we talk about on Dev Propulsion Labs comes back to trust and community.
