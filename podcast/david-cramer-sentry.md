# What David Cramer taught me about building for the Fortune 500,000

*Dev Propulsion Labs · December 2025*

I'm Victoria Melnikova, and I sat down with the founder of Sentry, the tool used by 4 million+ developers, and walked away rethinking what "enterprise" should mean for devtools companies.

- YouTube: https://youtu.be/wAx1IpJi6JI
- Show notes: https://evilmartians.com/events/david-cramer-founder-of-sentry-building-for-the-fortune-500-000

## Why Sentry builds for the Fortune 500,000

I talk to 40+ devtools startups a year, and the conversation almost always turns to one question: when do we go enterprise? David Cramer answers it with a phrase he borrowed from Atlassian, and I haven't stopped thinking about it since.

> "We build for the Fortune 500,000 instead of the Fortune 500. Every single decision we make flows from that."

Sentry hasn't raised prices in nearly a decade. They rejected FedRAMP and won't operate in China, and early on they turned away enterprise RFPs from insurance companies and banks. Many of those companies came back inbound when they were ready and became customers on Sentry's terms. I see the flip side with startups again and again: the ones who chase enterprise too early end up bending their product around a handful of accounts. David makes the product good and accessible enough that the enterprise comes to him.

## From Burger King to the Django ecosystem

David grew up in a small town in Nebraska. He worked at Burger King at 15 and spent summers detasseling corn, which means walking through cornfields in the heat, pulling the tops off the stalks. He couldn't afford a computer but was obsessed with them. When he finally got one, he taught himself to code through mIRC scripting, got into the Python and Django ecosystem, and started open-sourcing everything he built.

There was no growth strategy behind it. He published stuff because he could, and that brought him recognition, jobs, and conference talks. Sentry itself started as a side project while he worked at Disqus, and he built the hosting service over a Christmas break. When running Sentry alongside his Dropbox job became unbearable, he went full-time on Sentry in 2015. He was 30, on his sixth job.

What stuck with me most: David says Sentry is the kind of company it is because of a chip on his shoulder. He wanted validation and needed to prove they could do it entirely on their own, with no partnerships and every customer earned.

## How to delegate CEO and CTO, and still run the show

I asked David about delegation because I know it's a painful moment for many technical founders. He hired both a CEO and a CTO, roles he'd held himself, and his answer was pragmatic.

> "You gotta get to a point where 90% of the decisions they make would be the decisions you would've made. And then you gotta accept that some of them wouldn't be, and that's okay. Most of the time it's neutral. It doesn't matter."

Titles give you an illusion of control, and David would rather talk about influence. He has immense influence at Sentry without holding either title. His job hasn't changed much since he stepped out of both roles: he no longer deals with investors or exec hiring, but he's just as involved in strategy and engineering. What he gained is a larger peer group of smart people making decisions alongside him.

## Every customer Sentry has ever had came inbound

This one got me: every single customer came inbound. They've tried outbound, including a billboard with a CTO's name on it near John Deere's headquarters, and it hasn't worked yet. Sentry doesn't need it to.

The product is the default. Engineers adopt it bottom-up and bring it into their organizations, and that's how Disney Plus, banks, and major enterprises arrived. Even at their stage, they're still aggressively focused on YC-stage companies and the top of the funnel. Most companies Sentry's size would go upmarket, and David keeps going the other way.

## Stop founding companies at 20. Go learn from other people's mistakes

David's most contrarian advice is that 20-year-olds should stop founding companies. He worked at gaming companies, Disqus, and Dropbox before going full-time on Sentry at 30. His reasoning: at 20, you haven't seen enough people mess things up to know how to avoid it yourself.

> "Join early-stage companies, find interesting problems, work hard, build open source for recognition, and stop asking for permission."

He also thinks SF is still an unfair advantage for networking, and that instead of optimizing your career, you should find interesting things to work on. Among the founders I talk to on Dev Propulsion Labs, the ones who spent years in the trenches before starting their own thing tend to make sharper decisions, often enough that David's advice rings true for me.

## AI at Sentry: MCP, auto-fixes, and the end of the hype cycle

David built Sentry's MCP server himself, since he has the domain expertise to curate exactly the context an AI agent needs. It works, in his words, "unbelievably well." He's been coding exclusively through Claude Code and Cursor for six to nine months, without writing any code by hand.

He thinks the programming hype cycle is winding down and that models have stopped meaningfully improving. The difference between Codex and any Claude model is so small to him that he can't even tell. The real excitement, he says, is in image generation and creative tools like Nano Banana Pro, World Labs, and Suno for music.

At Sentry, though, AI is very practical. His hypothesis is that within a year or two, the easiest 10% of bugs will be auto-fixed with minimal human review, and he'd pay $200 per fix for that. The models aren't quite there yet, but he knows what he wants: a queue of proposed fixes for trivial bugs, ready to merge.

If I had to take one thing from this conversation, it's what David keeps coming back to: it's a game of focus. Find the thing that works, go after it until it stops working, and then find the next thing. It's much harder to do than to say.
