Dev Propulsion Labs · January 2026

What Monica Sarbu taught me about rebuilding a product from scratch and leading with people

Monica was on Dev Propulsion Labs three years ago, when Xata was positioned as "Airtable for databases." The company she brought back this time is a different one. We talked about the nerve it takes to rebuild, how a better question found the new product, and why people management is a superpower in developer tools.

The question that pointed Xata at developer velocity

This is the part I keep thinking about. When Monica's team was researching their pivot, they started by asking technical leaders: "What problems do you have with Postgres?" Most said none. Postgres is great, it performs well, no complaints.

Then they changed the question: "What's your biggest problem in the company?"

Every single leader gave the same answer: developer velocity.

"This kind of triggered us that there is something missing. We now have companies that come to us and say, 'Why has nobody done this in the last 10 years?'"

The new Xata is a modern Postgres platform that lets developers create database branches with anonymized production data for each feature or pull request, so the staging environment stops being a bottleneck. Xata competes on developer workflow instead of database performance, and that reframing came from one better question. I see the same pattern across the startups I work with: founders who ask about the customer's actual problem, rather than the one they want to solve, end up building something people need.

Rebuilding from scratch takes a specific kind of courage

Monica had a working product with users, the most generous free tier in the database market, and an established audience of builders and small companies. She decided to rebuild the platform from scratch anyway, with a new architecture, a new target audience, and a new pricing model without the free tier.

That takes guts, especially in the database market. It's intense in there: Supabase, PlanetScale, Neon, and actual feuds on the internet. Xata was never part of that noise and always did its own thing. After the pivot, it sits in a space nobody else really occupies: development workflow infrastructure rather than production database hosting.

Monica's advice on pivoting was practical: choose your audience. You go after either builders or SMBs and companies, and she doesn't think you can do both. Supabase is doing a great job with builders. Monica's team of senior engineers from Elastic gets more excited about complex features for larger-scale companies, so that's where Xata is placing its bet.

Europe is not a limitation anymore (but time zones still matter)

Monica is based in Berlin but spent six months in San Francisco to test being in the same time zone as customers. Her conclusion surprised me a bit: physical presence matters less than time zone coverage. Most customer interactions happen over Zoom anyway, even for companies based in SF.

She pointed to Lovable and Recraft as proof that you can stay in Europe and still build a global devtools company. More European founders are also splitting their time between continents, six months here and six months there. For Monica, having a daughter in the school system makes that back-and-forth harder.

I've been thinking about this a lot. A European founder doesn't need to uproot their life and move to SF anymore. What they need is people in the right time zones, a strong distributed culture, and the willingness to fly when it matters.

Why diverse teams build better products

Monica hires for complementary skills. If every engineer has the same background, the product reflects one perspective, while the audience is diverse. She's seen firsthand that gender-diverse teams produce better products, and nearly 40% of Xata's team is female, which is rare in developer tools.

"I learned that not only diversity in gender, but also diversity in education. In order to make a very good product, it matters. When you have a gender-diverse team, you tend to have better products."

Because Xata is remote-first, she only hires senior engineers. She doesn't want to set junior hires up for failure without in-person mentorship. She also looks for people who can drive projects by themselves: she's against handing engineers tasks and wants people who take ownership from day one.

This part felt personal. When I joined Evil Martians, I felt like my soft skills weren't needed in a deeply technical company. A colleague told me they were a culture add, and that the company needed those skills precisely because they didn't come naturally to the rest of the team. Monica had a similar moment when the CEO of Elastic told her she should be the people manager rather than the tech lead. Sometimes someone else spots your genius zone before you do.

Family offsites in Iceland, and the CEO who does one-on-ones with everyone

Xata meets twice a year for offsites and invites partners and kids. They've been to Iceland, Rome, and near Paris. The plus-ones organize their own activities while the team works on the roadmap, and by now everyone knows each other's families well. Monica says the family element strengthens the bonds.

Monica also does one-on-ones with every person at the company, adjusted to each individual's style. She starts at 6 AM because most of the engineering team is in Europe. She doesn't want people to think of her as a CEO. She'd rather be another teammate who happens to handle lawyers, branding, recruiting, and roadmap discussions in the same day.

What I took away from this episode is that Monica's superpower is people. She trusts everyone from day one and lets them lead, because she believes the trust to lead a project is often what brings out someone's potential. After so many founder conversations about technology, it was refreshing to hear someone talk about trust and people management. It seems to be working: Xata's team is growing, the product is getting great feedback, and companies keep asking why nobody has done this before.
