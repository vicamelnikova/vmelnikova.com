*Dev Propulsion Labs · January 2026*

I'm Victoria Melnikova, and on this episode of Dev Propulsion Labs, Piyush opened with a story about a friend who had been building a devtool startup for four years. When Piyush asked how he meets his customers, the friend said: "We go to conferences and we run into them." Four years in, he had no idea who his users were.

I see this problem constantly. I work with 40+ devtools startups a year, and a staggering number of them have millions of open-source users and still can't tell you who their customers are. Piyush built a company to close that gap.

## Go-to-market is the hardest unsolved problem in devtools

Piyush's thesis is that go-to-market for developer tools works differently from selling any other software, and most companies haven't adapted. Two decades ago, it was Yellow Pages selling. Someone would sell to the CTO, the tool got handed down, and seven out of ten times it sat on the shelf. There's even a word for it: shelf-ware.

Now developers influence the decisions. CTOs still sign the checks, but tools have become so vertically specialized that no CTO is an expert in every domain. A frontend background doesn't prepare you to evaluate DevOps tools, and a platform engineering background won't help you pick a data pipeline.

> "The whole crux of solving this go-to-market problem is figuring out who needs it. And if you can't figure that out, it's very, very hard to sell."

Try selling a database to a team that doesn't need one. Yet that's what most outbound does: spray and pray across thousands of companies without knowing which engineering teams are struggling with the problem your product solves.

## From 2% to 26% with intent signals

Reo.dev tracks developer behavior across 30+ platforms to spot the moments when an engineering team is struggling with a specific problem. Reach out before they need help and you get ignored; reach out four weeks late and you've missed the window. When Reo.dev detects intent at what Piyush calls the "moment of truth," the response rate jumps from low single digits to as high as 26%. That's one out of every four engineering buyers.

One of Reo.dev's early customers learned from its data that Tesla was using their open-source product. They'd had no idea, and they changed their whole marketing strategy after that. I know the feeling from Evil Martians: we've built over a hundred open-source projects, some are used by companies like Google or Apple, and we find out almost by accident.

## The first product was a Google Sheet

Before building anything, Piyush and his co-founders spent three months, eight hours a day, talking to prospective customers. About 85-90% strongly resonated with the problem. The first prototype was data sent to 10 customers in Google Sheets.

Those early customers didn't care about UI or UX, because they were seeing this data for the first time. That research became the backbone of everything Reo.dev built afterward.

I love this approach, because too many AI founders build without knowing what problem they're solving. Piyush's phrase for it is "Date the idea before you marry it." Spend serious time validating, talk to prospective customers relentlessly, and be ruthless about calling it when something isn't working.

## Even LangChain needs GTM help

I pushed back on this one. Why would fast-growing companies like LangChain or Temporal need go-to-market support when they're already growing like crazy?

Piyush's answer: enterprise users often run the open-source product while struggling with problems the paid product already solves. Nobody told them, and telling them is all it takes.

He's also watched founders change their minds over the last two years. In 2023, most devtool founders treated GTM as an afterthought. Now sustainability and go-to-market sit at the top of every founder's priority list. I believe open source should be commercialized to become sustainable, so hearing this matched what I see in the market every day.

## LLMs are becoming a real marketing channel

This part caught me off guard. Piyush says chatbots like ChatGPT and Claude are generating meaningful traffic for Reo.dev and their customers. If you're not thinking about LLM optimization today, you're already behind.

> "Good quality content has become so much more important. It doesn't matter what volume of content you're writing anymore. What matters is the quality and whether it's optimized for an LLM to quickly pick it up."

What works is original, high-quality content that LLMs can absorb as Q&A, plus an authentic Reddit presence. Derivative SEO content at volume doesn't work anymore. At Evil Martians, we've had leads come from Perplexity, ChatGPT, and Claude this year, so Piyush confirmed what I'd been suspecting: the companies that invest in quality content early are the ones showing up in AI responses.

When I asked Piyush what makes him feel great, he said it's seeing founders grow their businesses because of what Reo.dev does. As warm fuzzies go, that's a good one.
