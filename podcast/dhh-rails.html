What DHH taught me about staying unconventional

David Heinemeier Hansson, or DHH, created Ruby on Rails and co-founded Basecamp and HEY. He's also a best-selling author, a Le Mans class-winning racing driver, and one of the most opinionated people in tech. I'm Victoria Melnikova, and I sat down with him for Dev Propulsion Labs to talk about two decades of Rails, why he left the cloud, and what it means to build a company that refuses to follow the script.

Twenty years of Rails and still shipping

When DHH first extracted Ruby on Rails from Basecamp back in 2004, the idea that a single developer could build a full web application quickly was borderline radical. Convention over configuration and "don't repeat yourself" added up to a philosophy about how software should be built.

What strikes me about DHH is that he's as passionate about Rails now as he was two decades ago. Rails 7 brought Hotwire, Turbo, and a new way of thinking about frontend development without drowning in JavaScript frameworks. At Evil Martians, we've been deep in the Rails ecosystem for years, and I love watching it keep evolving.

The cloud exodus that nobody expected

DHH made headlines when Basecamp left the cloud. They were spending millions on AWS and realized they could own their hardware for a fraction of the long-term cost. Instead of quietly moving infrastructure, he wrote about it publicly and in detail, with the receipts.

"We saved over $1 million a year by leaving the cloud. And the performance is better."

DHH's point is narrower than the headlines: the default assumption that every company should be on AWS or Google Cloud deserves to be questioned. For companies with predictable workloads that have outgrown the elastic scaling phase, owning your hardware can make enormous financial sense. I've seen this with the startups I work with. The cloud is fantastic for getting started, but at scale the economics can flip.

Building a company that doesn't need venture capital

Basecamp has been profitable almost from the beginning, with no venture capital, no board pushing for hyper-growth, and no pressure to exit. DHH and Jason Fried built the kind of company most people in Silicon Valley would call impossible: calm, profitable, and sustainable for over two decades.

I asked about this because I constantly talk to devtools founders who feel trapped by the VC playbook: raise money, grow at all costs, find an exit. DHH shows you can build a meaningful technology company, pay people well, and ship great products without giving up control. The price is being comfortable staying smaller and resisting the growth-at-all-costs pressure that defines so much of the tech industry.

After talking to hundreds of startup founders, I find DHH's model more convincing than ever, because companies built this way tend to still be around a decade later. Many of the ones that chased growth at all costs aren't.

Opinionated software for opinionated people

Rails is famously opinionated. It tells you where your files go, how your database maps to your objects, and what your URL structure should look like. Some developers hate this, and DHH doesn't care.

"If you try to please everyone, you end up pleasing no one. Make strong choices and let people opt out if they disagree."

The same philosophy shows up in HEY, the email service Basecamp launched, which rethought email from scratch. It has no labels and no auto-sorting, and it makes you screen new senders before they can reach your inbox. People feel strongly about it both ways, which is what you get with a product that has a point of view.

I think this is one of the most underrated lessons for devtools founders. The temptation is to support every use case and never alienate anyone. Real loyalty goes to products that make strong choices, like Rails and HEY. The developers who love those products really love them.

The race car driver who writes code

DHH is also a Le Mans class-winning racing driver, and I bring it up because it says something about how he approaches everything. Racing at Le Mans takes years of preparation, physical conditioning, and a willingness to push yourself to the limit while managing enormous risk.

He brings the same intensity to technology. When he decided to leave the cloud, he documented the entire journey and turned it into a movement. He testified before Congress when he disagreed with Apple's App Store policies, and he wrote multiple books about remote work years before COVID proved him right.

What founders can take from this is that conviction creates momentum. You don't have to be right about everything, but you do have to believe in something strongly enough to do it publicly and deal with the consequences. DHH has been doing that for twenty years.

Why this conversation matters for devtools builders

This was one of my earliest episodes of Dev Propulsion Labs, and it set the tone for the show. DHH has been building for over twenty years. He has stayed independent, kept strong opinions in an industry that loves consensus, and he keeps shipping.

For the devtools founders I talk to every week, the lesson is this: you don't have to play the game everyone else is playing. You can be profitable without VC money, own your infrastructure, build opinionated software that not everyone likes, and take strong public stances and survive the backlash, as long as you stay consistent over time.

Rails, Basecamp, and HEY are all still here. In an industry where most products don't survive five years, twenty years of relevance is a strong argument, and DHH isn't slowing down.
