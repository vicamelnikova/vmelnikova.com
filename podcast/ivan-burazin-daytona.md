# What Ivan Burazin taught me about walking away from revenue to bet on agents

*Dev Propulsion Labs · November 2025*

I'm Victoria Melnikova, and on this episode of Dev Propulsion Labs I sat down with a founder who has been building cloud development environments since 2009, a full decade before the market was ready. When the AI agent wave hit, he went all in: he walked away from $300K ARR on New Year's Eve and rebuilt everything from scratch.

- YouTube: https://youtu.be/6WCwO_oGsBY
- Show notes: https://evilmartians.com/events/ivan-burazin-ceo-of-daytona-walking-from-300k-arr-to-build-for-agents

I first connected with Ivan back in 2023, when Daytona was just an idea and he was still figuring things out. Now Daytona is doubling revenue every month, buying servers at a pace that would make your head spin, and drowning in master service agreements. I still can't get over how fast that happened.

## Composable computers for agents (not sandboxes)

Ivan thinks the word "sandbox" undersells what Daytona builds. What they actually make are composable computers for AI agents. At creation time, an agent can pick CPUs, RAM, disk, GPU, and even the operating system (Linux, Windows, or Mac), so it gets a full computer it can configure on demand rather than just a safe place to execute code.

The scale blew my mind. Take ChatGPT: millions of users, each interaction a new agent, and each agent possibly spinning up a computer. Now multiply that across every company building agents. Ivan's estimate is that the number of agents will be the number of humans to the power of ten.

> "The number of agents will essentially be the number of humans to the power of ten. They will need this new primitive to be able to get their job done."

Plenty of founders pitch big numbers, but Ivan is already running millions of sandboxes, with revenue covering most costs, on a version of the product that launched just six months before we sat down.

## Selling to the parent and the child

Ivan gave me the clearest analogy I've heard for the dual-audience problem in agentic infrastructure: it's like selling children's toys. The parent (the developer) has to choose the product, and the child (the agent) has to perform well inside it.

Agents can't yet discover and adopt infrastructure on their own, so developers still make the buying decision. Once the agent runs, though, performance matters, and Ivan says agents are twice as productive on Daytona as on alternatives. When things break, he gets on calls himself, and the team responds in minutes.

I work with 40+ devtools startups a year, and support quality is one of the most underrated competitive advantages I see. Ivan gets this instinctively. Even when Daytona screws up, the speed and honesty of the response keeps customers around: every customer who had a problem stayed.

## Revenue goes to zero on New Year's Eve

Daytona used to do dev environment automation for human developers and large enterprises, and it was at $300K ARR, which was not bad at all pre-AI. But by late 2024, inbound requests from agent builders started showing up.

On New Year's Eve, Ivan and his co-founder built a beta without telling the rest of the company. When early users confirmed it was directionally right, they made the call to let revenue go to zero, thank every old customer, and rebuild from scratch starting February 1st.

> "Should have done it sooner. It's always like, you should have done that sooner."

They hit the old $300K ARR in the first month of the new product and kept doubling from there. Ivan had learned about timing the hard way. His first company, Codeanywhere, was a browser-based IDE built in 2009, a decade before Replit and Codespaces, and being that early didn't help. This time he knew in his gut that the timing was right.

## Play to your genius zone

Daytona's go-to-market is almost entirely in-person events. Ivan built and sold a conference company before, so meetups, hackathons, and drinks are second nature for his team. Two people on the GTM team came from that company and have organized events in stadiums, so a San Francisco meetup is nothing for them.

Ivan is honest about what he's not good at. He can't write: it takes too long and doesn't feel natural. Getting in a room with people is his genius zone, and since almost the entire market of agent builders is in San Francisco, the geography works in his favor.

They promoted the product for three months before it even launched. In hindsight, Ivan calls that one of their smartest moves, but at the time it was a leap of faith, and I respect that kind of conviction.

## 19 years with the same people, racing on a moving train

Ivan has worked with his co-founders for nearly two decades across multiple companies. Twelve of Daytona's 18 employees are people they've worked with for 6 to 20 years, and the communication is direct and high-fidelity, with zero BS.

> "My co-founder will call me and say, stop talking shit, this is a dumb idea. Or vice versa. There's no fuzziness around it. It's very direct."

That kind of communication matters when you're doubling every month. But Ivan says growing fast isn't enough: you have to outrun the market itself. In his analogy, you're on a moving train (the AI wave), racing competitors on that train, and when the market slows down, only number one survives.

When I asked what makes him feel great, Ivan surprised me by talking about time. He sees Daytona as a way to buy it back: agents take work off humans so they can spend more time with their kids, or on whatever matters most to them. Coming from someone who admits he hopes to see his kids in the morning and at least put them to bed at night, that answer hit home.
